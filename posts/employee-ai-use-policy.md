If you haven't given your team guidance on AI, they're probably using it anyway, on personal accounts, with no rules about what information goes in. A short, plain-English AI use policy fixes that without slowing anyone down.

The goal isn't to scare people away from AI. It's to make it safe and normal to use it well.

## What a good policy covers

Keep it to one page. Employees should be able to read it in five minutes and remember the main points.

1. **Why we use AI.** One or two sentences: we want AI to save time on routine work so people can focus on customers.
2. **Approved tools.** List the specific tools and accounts employees may use for work, and say that personal AI accounts shouldn't be used for company information.
3. **Data rules.** The heart of the policy. Spell out what must never go into AI tools that aren't approved for it.
4. **Human review.** Anything AI helps create that goes to a customer, or is used to make a decision about a person, must be checked by an employee first.
5. **Honesty.** Don't present AI output as verified fact without checking it, and follow any disclosure rules in your industry.
6. **Questions and mistakes.** Who to ask, and what to do if someone shares something they shouldn't have.

## A simple template

> **[Company] AI Use Policy**
>
> **Purpose.** We use AI tools to save time on routine work. These rules keep our customers' and our company's information safe.
>
> **Approved tools.** For company work, use only: [list tools and account type]. Don't use personal AI accounts for company information.
>
> **Never enter into an unapproved tool:** customer personal information (names with contact details, Social Security numbers, payment or bank details, health information), passwords or access codes, employee records, non-public financials, or anything a client asked us to keep confidential.
>
> **Always review.** Check AI output for accuracy before using it. Anything sent to a customer must be reviewed by you first. AI may not make hiring, firing, pricing, or credit decisions on its own.
>
> **Be transparent.** If asked whether AI helped with something, answer honestly.
>
> **Questions or mistakes.** Ask [name]. If you think sensitive information went into the wrong tool, tell [name] right away. Reporting quickly is what matters.
>
> Last updated: [date]

## Tips for rolling it out

- **Pair it with training.** A policy alone won't build skills. Show people good uses for their actual jobs.
- **Give them a good tool.** If the approved option is worse than what's free on their phones, people will drift back. A business-tier subscription is often worth it.
- **Review it twice a year.** AI tools change fast. Update the approved list as you go.
- **Make it blame-free to report.** You want to hear about mistakes early.

## Going further

For a deeper framework on managing AI risk, the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes the [AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework). It's written for larger organizations, but its ideas scale down well.
